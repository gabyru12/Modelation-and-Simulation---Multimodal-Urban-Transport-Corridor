# How a Vehicle Decides: Driver Behaviour in SUMO

## Decisions are layered, and the slowest constraint wins

A simulated driver in SUMO does not run a single behavioural algorithm. It runs several largely independent decision layers, each of which proposes a constraint, and the vehicle's behaviour in a time step is the combination of these constraints under a simple principle: for speed, the **minimum of all upper bounds wins**, and for lateral movement, a lane change is carried out only if some motivation wants it and the safety checks permit it. Keeping this layering in mind turns an apparently opaque simulator into a predictable one. When a vehicle behaves strangely, the question to ask is which layer imposed the constraint that it is obeying: its own desired speed, the car in front, an upcoming junction, a traffic light, a stop, a lane-change manoeuvre, or an external controller.

The layers are best understood in the order in which they are consulted. First the vehicle determines how fast it would like to go if nothing were in its way. Second it applies the **car-following model** against its leader. Third it considers every object ahead that demands a speed change: junctions, signals, stops, closed or ending lanes. Fourth, separately, it decides whether to change lane, and fifth, over longer time scales, whether to change route. A last group of influences (friction, devices, TraCI overrides) modifies the inputs to these layers rather than adding new ones.

## Layer one: how fast does the driver want to go?

The free-driving target speed is the minimum of three quantities: the vehicle type's `maxSpeed`, which stands for the capability of the engine and chassis, the vehicle type's `desiredMaxSpeed` multiplied by the individual `speedFactor`, and the **speed limit of the lane multiplied by the same speed factor**. The speed factor is drawn per vehicle from a distribution (default mean 1.0, deviation 0.1), which is how SUMO models a population in which some drivers habitually exceed the limit and others undershoot it. Anything that changes the effective limit therefore changes the target: variable speed signs and calibrators override lane limits, vehicle-class speed restrictions on edge types apply to specific classes, lane or edge `speed` attributes can be changed by TraCI, and the friction device lowers the target on slippery surfaces by a factor computed from a lane's friction coefficient, perturbed by driver-specific noise. The GLOSA device can temporarily raise or lower the speed factor to hit a green phase, and the elevation-aware KraussPS model reduces the speed on slopes when `desiredMaxSpeed` is lower than `maxSpeed`. Turning movements inside junctions are limited by a speed derived from the turn radius, so curvature, though not modelled continuously, still slows vehicles through bends in the network.

## Layer two: following a leader

The default **Krauss model**, designed by Stefan Krauß, has a simple structure that is worth understanding because it explains much of SUMO's behaviour. Each step, the follower computes the highest speed at which it could still stop safely behind its leader if the leader braked as hard as it can, given its own reaction time. This **safe speed** grows with the gap between the two vehicles and with the leader's speed, and shrinks with the follower's desired headway. The follower then picks the minimum of that safe speed, the speed it could reach by accelerating at its maximum rate, and its target speed from layer one. Finally it applies **dawdling**: a random reduction of the speed by up to `sigma` times the maximum acceleration. The `sigma` parameter, between zero and one with a default of 0.5, is what makes simulated traffic spontaneously form stop-and-go waves at high density, and setting it to zero makes driving perfectly smooth and the road as full as it can be.

The behaviour of this layer is determined by a small number of vehicle-type parameters. `tau` is the desired minimum time headway (default one second) and is the dominant term in road capacity at higher speeds. `minGap` is the gap kept when standing (default 2.5 metres) and, together with vehicle length, dominates capacity at low speed. `accel` limits acceleration, `decel` is the comfortable braking rate used when planning, `apparentDecel` is the braking rate the follower *assumes its leader is capable of*, which defaults to `decel`, and `emergencyDecel` is the maximum physically possible braking used when the situation has become critical. A follower that finds itself closer than the safe gap but able to avoid a crash will brake harder than `decel`, up to `emergencyDecel`, which is how emergency braking arises in a simulation without any explicit panic logic. Making `tau` smaller than the step length is dangerous, since the model can then not react in time and rear-end collisions result in normal driving.

Other models change the details but not the layering. **KraussPS** adds slope awareness, and the **IDM** family computes an acceleration from the desired speed, the gap and the speed difference with a smooth interaction term, so vehicles approach gaps asymptotically. The **EIDM** extension reproduces realistic drive-off profiles and subsecond dynamics and needs steps of 0.05 to 0.5 seconds. The **Wiedemann** model uses psycho-physical thresholds and regimes (free driving, approaching, following, braking) with its parameters `cc1` to `cc9`. **ACC** and **CACC** model adaptive cruise control with four modes (speed control, gap control, gap closing, collision avoidance) and a minimal-gap time-headway rule, and the ACC documentation warns that it can collide at the default one-second step. The **Rail** model describes trains by traction and resistance curves. The model is selected per vehicle type with `carFollowModel`, so one simulation can mix, for instance, human drivers using Krauss and automated vehicles using CACC.

A decoupling that deserves emphasis is the **action step length**. Normally every vehicle recomputes its decision each simulation step. With an action step length of, say, 0.5 seconds, the driver acts only at multiples of that interval and holds its acceleration in between. This models reaction time and perception delay without the cost of a smaller global step, and it implies that `tau` must not be smaller than the action step length either.

## Layer three: obstacles ahead

Besides the leader, a driver continuously looks ahead for things that require it to slow down or stop, and each produces a speed constraint of the form "be able to stop here". The most important is the next **junction link**. Whether the vehicle needs to stop depends on the right-of-way state of the link, on the signal if there is one, on conflicting traffic, and on what lies beyond.

For a link with priority, in the jargon a major link, the vehicle simply drives through without slowing. For a link without priority, a minor link, the vehicle decelerates towards the stop line until it reaches a visibility distance (configurable per connection) at which it can see the foe streams, then judges whether any foe vehicle would be disturbed. In the SUMO model a foe is a vehicle on a conflicting link that either has priority or is already within the junction, and the judgement is made on **time gaps**: the vehicle will enter only if it can pass ahead of or behind each foe with at least a minimum gap, `jmTimegapMinor`, and it can also be willing to risk a smaller gap as it grows **impatient**. The `impatience` parameter and the waiting time increase willingness to push in, and `jmIgnoreFoeProb` and `jmIgnoreFoeSpeed` let a fraction of drivers ignore foes entirely, whereas `jmIgnoreJunctionFoeProb` does so for foes that are already inside the intersection. Pedestrians on a crossing are respected up to `jmCrossingGap` metres away. `jmSigmaMinor` replaces `sigma` for the imperfection while passing a minor link, `jmStoplineGap` is the distance at which the vehicle stops from a stop line, and `jmAdvance` decides whether the vehicle may advance to a waiting point inside the junction when a foe's trajectory crosses its own.

Right-of-way is decided structurally from the junction type described in [02-network-building.md](02-network-building.md). `Priority` junctions rank approaches by edge priority and speed, `right_before_left` gives way to the right, `allway_stop` makes every vehicle stop and take turns, `zipper` makes merging vehicles alternate (starting to adapt about a hundred metres before the merge), and `unregulated` performs no checks, so collisions are possible. Entering is also subject to the **keep-clear** heuristic: a vehicle will not enter a junction if the exit appears blocked and it would block the junction in turn, unless it has already waited longer than `jmIgnoreKeepClearTime`.

For a **traffic light**, the decision depends on the signal character of the link. Green with priority (`G`) allows passage, lowercase green (`g`) allows passage only if conflicting streams are free, red stops the vehicle, and yellow lets the vehicle stop if it can do so with its comfortable deceleration and otherwise drive through. A vehicle that has been too close to stop at the moment of the change uses stronger braking if the yellow phase proves too short for its speed. Behavioural variation is added by `jmDriveAfterYellowTime` and `jmDriveAfterRedTime`, which let a vehicle violate a signal that changed less than a given time ago, with `jmDriveRedSpeed` limiting the speed while doing so, and by the blue-light device, which allows emergency vehicles to ignore red altogether.

Other obstacles follow the same pattern. A **stop** (bus stop, parking area, scheduled stop on a lane) imposes a stopping point and duration, and public transport vehicles approach with a deceleration profile and may be delayed by passengers boarding for `boardingDuration` each. A lane that ends, a lane closed by a rerouter, a vehicle of another class parked in the lane, or the end of the route impose similar constraints.

## Layer four: lane changing

Lane changing is a separate decision made after the longitudinal one and organised, in the standard model **LC2013**, around four motivations considered in a priority order. The **strategic** motivation is the necessity to change lane in order to follow the route: if the next edge in the route is reachable only from another lane, the driver looks ahead a distance depending on speed and on `lcStrategic` and begins moving early enough to arrive in the right lane, with urgency increasing as the distance to the required exit decreases. The **cooperative** motivation, governed by `lcCooperative`, makes a driver give space to a vehicle that needs to enter its lane, by changing lane or adapting its speed. The **speed gain** motivation, governed by `lcSpeedGain`, makes the driver change to a neighbouring lane when it expects to travel faster there, estimated from the speeds of the leaders on each lane over a look-ahead time. `lcSpeedGainRight` makes changes to the right less demanding than changes to the left, which together with the **keep right** motivation, `lcKeepRight`, produces the keep-right rule of lane discipline, while `lcOvertakeRight` is the probability of tolerating overtaking on the right. The strategic motivation overrides the others when it becomes urgent, and the cooperative and speed-gain motivations are suppressed when they would compromise the strategic need.

Having a motivation is not enough. The change must pass **safety checks** on the target lane, which require sufficient gaps to the new leader and follower given their speeds, and the willingness to accept small gaps is raised by `lcAssertive` and increased by `lcImpatience`, which grows while a desired change is blocked over the time `lcTimeToImpatience`. Permissions also constrain the decision, because a vehicle may not enter a lane that does not allow its class, and the `changeLeft` and `changeRight` attributes on lanes can restrict which classes may cross a lane boundary. If the network lacks a lane-to-lane connection a driver needs, the outcome is the "unexpected" late or forced lane change mentioned in [13-common-problems.md](13-common-problems.md).

With the **sublane model** (`--lateral-resolution`), a vehicle has a continuous lateral position and speed in place of discrete lanes. It has a preferred lateral alignment (`latAlignment`), a maximum lateral speed (`maxSpeedLat`), a minimum lateral gap, an eagerness to use its alignment (`lcSublane`), and a willingness to encroach laterally on other drivers (`lcPushy`, `lcPushyGap`), and imperfection in lateral keeping (`lcSigma`). It permits motorcycles and bicycles to filter within a lane and underlies rescue-lane formation. **Opposite-direction driving**, enabled by neighbour information in the network, allows a vehicle to overtake slow traffic by using the oncoming lane when there is no oncoming traffic in a look-ahead of up to 150 metres, with eagerness `lcOpposite`.

## Layer five: route choice

A vehicle drives the route it was given unless something changes it. The rerouting device lets it recompute its route at a configurable period using the current average edge speeds, and a vehicle will do so only with a given probability and, optionally, only if the new route is significantly faster. A **rerouter** object triggers a route change when the vehicle reaches a particular edge, and may close edges, assign new destinations by probability, or assign new routes. The vehicle's response depends on whether the closure is hard or soft, on whether alternatives exist, on how early the sign is passed, and on how the vehicle was defined (as a trip, with `via` edges, or as a fixed route). For parking, a vehicle whose parking area is full searches for an alternative according to distance, occupancy and visibility. Persons choose among modes with the intermodal router, and taxis accept or refuse reservations according to the dispatch algorithm. A TraCI client can replace all of this by calling `setRoute` or `changeTarget` at any time.

## Inputs that modify every layer

Many influences act by modifying parameters rather than adding a decision. Vehicle **devices**, which are optional per-vehicle components, are the most flexible mechanism: the driver-state device adds perception errors and reaction delays, the take-over-control device models the hand-over between automation and human driving, the surrogate-safety device measures time-to-collision, the blue-light device gives emergency privileges, the bluetooth devices make vehicles detectable, and the stationfinder, GLOSA, friction and taxi devices implement the features described in [11-additional-features.md](11-additional-features.md). **Generic parameters** attached to objects can change model settings through XML or TraCI at runtime. **Randomness** enters through `sigma`, the speed-factor draw, the probability-based flow generation, the choice among type and route distributions, the lateral and junction imperfections and the random assignment of devices, all drawn from seeded generators so that runs are reproducible. Finally an **external controller** via TraCI can override any layer according to the speed mode and lane-change mode bitsets, and this is the intended way of testing a decision rule that SUMO does not implement.

## Putting it together: a worked narrative

Consider a car approaching a signalised intersection on the right-hand lane with a bus ahead. Its target speed is the lane limit times its personal factor. The car-following layer computes a safe speed behind the bus, probably lower, and the car slows. The look-ahead layer notes the traffic light on the connection towards the next edge of its route, finds that it is green with priority, and imposes no constraint. A few hundred metres earlier, the strategic lane-change motivation observed that the route requires a left turn which is only possible from the left lane, so the car looked for a gap and began a lane change, which the safety check allowed only after the follower on the target lane had passed. In the left lane the junction link is a left turn that conflicts with oncoming traffic, so the car approaches the stop line and, from the visibility distance, evaluates the time gaps to the oncoming vehicles. It enters the internal waiting point, waits for a sufficient gap while its impatience grows, and when the signal turns yellow decides according to its remaining distance whether it can stop and otherwise finishes the turn. Each of these decisions is a separate parameterised rule, and each can be inspected through the output and TraCI facilities described elsewhere.
