# Road Structures and Networks

## Where a network can come from

A SUMO network can be conjured up in four broad ways. It can be **imported** from real-world data: OpenStreetMap is the favourite, with a web wizard that builds a runnable scenario from a map selection in a few clicks, but conversions also exist for VISUM and Vissim models, OpenDRIVE, MATsim, ArcView shapefiles, DLR-Navteq road databases and RoboCup rescue maps. It can be **written by hand** in a handful of plain XML files for nodes, edges, connections, road types and signals. It can be **drawn graphically** in the netedit editor, which can also modify any imported network. Or it can be **generated abstractly** as a grid of any size, a spider web of arms and rings, or a random network with controllable density, which is ideal for controlled experiments in which real geometry would be a confounding factor. Networks can later be patched, merged, compared, filtered, exported to plain XML, OpenDRIVE, MATsim, DLR-Navteq and Amitran formats, and converted to GeoJSON or KML for GIS tools.

## Geometry and topology

Within a network, the vocabulary is rich. Roads can have any number of lanes in each direction, with individual lane widths, speed limits and permitted vehicle classes, and lanes can start, end, split and merge, so that turn pockets, acceleration and deceleration lanes, lane drops and auxiliary lanes are all expressible. Edges can follow curved polyline geometry in three dimensions, so hills, bridges and tunnels at different heights can be represented, with elevation imported from height maps, shapefiles or geotiffs. Junctions can have any number of arms with any angles, and a junction's shape and its internal lanes are computed automatically. Roundabouts, including multi-lane ones, are recognised from the data or declared explicitly. Roads can have a spread type that determines whether lanes flare to the right, to the centre or around a road centre, and edges can be given stop offsets that let stopping lines for certain vehicle classes sit further back, producing bike boxes and advanced stop lines. Coordinates can be geographic, so that networks line up with satellite imagery, and positions can be referenced linearly along a road as kilometrage.

## Junction regulation

The right of way at a junction can be configured as a simple priority junction, a right-before-left junction, an all-way stop, a signalised junction, a zipper merge in which streams interleave one by one, or an unregulated junction with no safety logic at all, which is useful for abstract or highly controlled scenarios. Individual connections can be fine-tuned: a movement can be given absolute priority, forced to yield, given a custom crossing speed, a particular visibility distance or a keep-clear behaviour, and explicit prohibitions can say that one specific movement must wait for another. Lane-to-lane connections can be inferred automatically from the lane counts, or specified exactly, which allows exclusive turn lanes, shared lanes, lane-specific permissions and the weaving sections of motorway interchanges. Turnarounds can be enabled or suppressed junction by junction.

## Special-purpose infrastructure

Beyond ordinary roads, networks can include bus lanes and tram tracks, bicycle lanes, sidewalks, pedestrian crossings and walking areas at junctions, railway tracks including bidirectional ones with signals and switches, motorway ramps and weaving sections, waterways for ships, and parking spaces. Opposite-direction information lets vehicles overtake via the oncoming lane on rural roads. Left-hand traffic is a single switch. Roads can carry **vehicle-class restrictions** at the lane level, so that a single edge may offer a bus lane, two general lanes, a taxi-only lane and a sidewalk, and individual lanes can forbid lane changes to or from them for chosen classes.

## Real-world heuristics

When importing from messy data, netconvert can reconstruct what the data lack: it can guess ramps and acceleration lanes, join clusters of nearby junctions into one, guess traffic-light locations and generate signal programs for them, merge parallel streets, add sidewalks or bike lanes from road speeds or tags, interpret turn-lane markings, remove unneeded geometry nodes, filter by vehicle class, road type or minimum speed, and keep or drop edges by explicit lists. Typemaps for urban, rural, pedestrian, bicycle, ship and railway scenarios specialise this behaviour, so the same raw map can be turned into very different simulations.
