# Demand and Routing Options

## Ways to say "traffic exists"

Demand in SUMO can be specified at several levels of precision, and a scenario may mix them. At the most explicit level, individual vehicles are listed with their exact departure time and complete route. A step up, **trips** state only where and when a vehicle starts and ends, and the router fills in the path. **Flows** say that vehicles of a given type are emitted between two places at a given rate over a time window, whether at fixed intervals, at a number per hour, at a probability per second (a binomial arrival process) or with exponentially distributed gaps (a Poisson process), or for a fixed total number. Routes can be given as lists of edges, as distributions of alternative routes with probabilities, or implied by origin and destination junctions, traffic zones or even map-matched coordinates, and a trip can pass through waypoints. Whole scenarios can be scaled up or down with a single factor.

## Generating demand from data

SUMO has an unusual number of ways to turn real-world data into traffic. **Origin-destination matrices**, in the common VISUM and Vissim formats or as XML, become trips between traffic analysis zones, with time slicing, daily profiles, scaling and type assignment. **Detector counts** from induction loops become routes and flows through dedicated tools for motorway networks, and a route sampler can pick from a pool of candidate routes so as to reproduce measured edge counts and turning counts. **Turning percentages** at junctions can drive vehicles through the network with no destinations at all, which suits intersection-focused studies. **Population statistics**, including age brackets, employment, school and workplace locations and car ownership, can generate commuting, leisure and through-traffic by foot, bus and car using an activity-based generator. **Public-transport timetables** in the GTFS format are imported with their stops, lines and trips, optionally following OSM route geometry. For quick tests there are **random trips**, with options to favour long roads or fast roads, to concentrate origins and destinations on the network fringe to mimic through-traffic, to generate buses or pedestrians instead of cars, and to produce intermodal person trips. A distribution generator can create a whole heterogeneous fleet from parameter ranges, and netedit lets demand be drawn directly on the map.

## Routing choices

For a given trip, the router can compute the **fastest path** by travel time, the **shortest path** by distance, or a path minimising an arbitrary effort such as emissions, energy, cost or a priority-weighted combination. It can use free-flow times, travel times measured in an earlier run, time-varying weights, or user-supplied weight files. It can repair or discard unreachable routes, route between junctions or zones, and choose among several candidate destinations. A palette of algorithms trades speed against flexibility, from the exact Dijkstra to landmark-accelerated A\*, and contraction-hierarchy variants for routing huge numbers of trips, including per-vehicle-class and weight-updatable versions. **Dynamic user assignment** iterates between routing and simulating so that drivers spread across alternatives and approach an equilibrium, using Gawron or logit choice models and several convergence strategies, and **one-shot assignment** achieves similar effects by rerouting vehicles as they depart.

## Routing during the simulation

Routes do not have to be fixed in advance. Vehicles can carry a **rerouting device** that recomputes their route periodically from current traffic conditions, with a configurable share of the fleet equipped, a configurable period, smoothing of observed speeds, a threshold for how much better a route must be, random distortion of weights for diversity, and optional use of traffic zones as destinations. Vehicles can also be rerouted when they reach particular edges, by closures, by information signs that reassign destinations or routes with given probabilities, by parking-availability signs, or by an external program through the control interface. A vehicle can be told to change target, to follow a new route, to insert or replace a stop, or to reroute by travel time or by effort at any moment.

## Multimodal routing

Persons are routed across modes. A person's trip can be left abstract, with only an origin, a destination and the allowed modes, and the router assembles walk, ride, wait and transfer stages across walking, public transport, private car, bicycle and taxi, using costs for walking speed, waiting, access to stops and mode-switch locations such as parking areas. Trip chains with activities of given duration between movements are supported, as are persons who start inside a vehicle, groups of persons sharing a plan, and flows of persons. Containers can be routed in the same spirit between container stops.
